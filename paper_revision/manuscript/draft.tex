\begin{abstract}
Text classifiers used to filter misinformation are vulnerable to adversarial examples, but most existing defences require white-box access to the victim model. We present the first systematic comparison of black-box preprocessing defences for credibility assessment, evaluating twelve techniques across four tasks, three victim architectures, and five attackers --- including the adaptive reinforcement-learning attacker. We then introduce MACABEU, a reinforcement-learning meta-defender that selects which static defence to deploy for a given example, in both an offline (frozen-policy) and an online (continually-updated) regimes. Our experiments show that simple static defences, when chosen well, already neutralise non-adaptive attackers; that adaptive attackers re-establish a substantial threat against any single static defence; and that an adaptive defender recovers most of the lost robustness without modifying the victim. \hl{The best MACABEU variant reduces the efficiency of the attacks by 10--40\% relative to static defences, with improvements on every one of 12 task--victim combinations, even against an adaptive attacker.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Text classification models based on neural networks have been increasingly popular due to high decision accuracy, but they suffer from vulnerability to \textit{adversarial examples} (AEs). This term describes pieces of data (i.e.\ text fragments) that have been purposefully modified by an attacker to elicit incorrect decisions from a victim classifier \citep{Zhang2020b}. Credibility assessment of user-generated text, e.g.\ within content filtering for a social network, is a good example where robustness to such attacks plays a key role \citep{Carter2021}.

Previous research \cite{verifying_robustness} has shown that, indeed, often a small change made to the non-credible input (e.g.\ a fake-news article) can confuse a misinformation detector to rate it as credible. AEs are especially effective when the attacker is \textit{adaptive}, i.e.\ has had the opportunity to learn the victim's vulnerabilities through previous interaction \citep{przybyla-etal-2024-know}.

Less attention has been given to how to design \textit{defences} against such attacks. Many classical defences against AEs involve special modifications to the victim classifier, e.g.\ adding AEs to the training data or modifying the loss function \citep{Goyal2023survey}. However, the most effective text classifiers used currently are based on large language models, often \textit{very} large and executed as a service. Their users typically do not have access to the model's weights, the computational capacity, or the expertise to perform custom training.

\hl{Therefore, in this work we investigate available \textit{black-box defences}: techniques that make the victim classifier more robust without any changes to the model itself. Specifically, we answer the following research questions in the context of misinformation detection:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \hl{\textbf{RQ1}: Which existing black-box defences provide the best robustness across attacks, text domains, and victim classifiers?}
    \item \hl{\textbf{RQ2}: How do these static defences fare against an adaptive attacker --- both one pre-trained on the original victim and re-trained against a known defence?}
    \item \hl{\textbf{RQ3}: Can a defender itself be made adaptive, choosing the most effective defence per example or even continuing to learn during the attack?}
\end{enumerate}

\hl{To address RQ3 we introduce \textbf{MACABEU} (\textbf{MA}king \textbf{C}redibility \textbf{A}ssessment ro\textbf{B}ust when \textbf{E}valuated \textbf{U}nder attacks), a reinforcement-learning meta-defender that selects, on a per-example basis, which existing defence from a fixed pool to deploy.} MACABEU operates in an \emph{offline} mode, in which the policy is frozen at deployment, and \hl{two \emph{online} modes} in which the policy continues to update from real attacker queries observed at evaluation time. The latter are, to our knowledge, the first defenders from textual AEs that adapt \emph{during} an attack.

To encourage further research in this direction, we share the source code used for these experiments\footnote{\hl{Attached to the submission for review; when published available at} \url{https://github.com/[ANONYMOUS]/macabeu}}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}
\paragraph{Adversarial examples for text.}
Since the seminal observation that small, semantics-preserving perturbations can fool neural classifiers \citep{Goodfellow2015, Szegedy2014}, a large body of work has explored AE generation for text. Attacks operate at different granularities: character-level (DeepWordBug \citep{Gao2018}, VIPER \citep{Eger2019}), word-level (TextFooler \citep{Jin2020}, BERT-Attack \citep{Li2020bert}, PWWS \citep{Ren2019}), and sentence-level paraphrasing \citep{Iyyer2018}. Search strategies range from greedy saliency-based replacement to gradient-free black-box optimisation such as genetic algorithms \citep{Alzantot2018} and particle swarm \citep{Zang2020}. Recent work has explored LLM-driven attacks that generate fluent rewrites in a single pass \citep{Moraffah2024purification, ZouUniversalLLM2023}. Toolkits such as OpenAttack \citep{Zeng2021} and TextAttack \citep{Morris2020} make these methods readily reproducible.


\paragraph{Robustness of misinformation detectors.}
Text credibility assessment \citep{Srba2026credibility} is an application in particular danger of AEs, e.g. an attacker who tricks a fake-news classifier can disseminate manipulated content at scale. \citet{verifying_robustness} introduced BODEGA, a benchmark covering four credibility tasks (propaganda, fact-checking, hyperpartisan news, rumour detection) and a composite metric balancing attack success with semantic and character-level similarity. Follow-up work has shown that adaptive attackers, which learn the victim's behaviour during interaction, can be substantially more effective than fixed heuristics; XARELLO \citep{przybyla-etal-2024-know} formalises this as reinforcement learning over a bandit of perturbation operators with a Transformer-based $Q$-estimator. Subsequent work has extended this picture to LLM-generated adversarial inputs against fake-news and fact-checking classifiers \citep{Bethany2025camouflage, Aldahoul2025multiagent, Przybyla2025LLMattack}, and recent surveys catalogue both the attack surface for automated fact-checking \citep{Liu2025fcsurvey} and the broader credibility-signal landscape under LLMs \citep{Srba2026credibility}, confirming that credibility classifiers remain brittle outside the original BODEGA setup.

\paragraph{Threat models.}
The two most important aspects of the threat model are the level of access to the victim model enjoyed by the attackers and defenders. In terms of \textbf{attacker's} access, the \textit{white-box} model assumes full access to the victims' weights, allowing gradient attacks such as FGSM \citep{Goodfellow2015} and PGD \citep{Madry2018}. However, it is not realistic to assume such access on the attackers' part. Previous work in misinformation domain focused on the scenario labelled \textit{black-box} (only final decisions are visible) or \textit{grey-box} (a continuous probability score is visible). Concrete examples include label-only black-box search such as PWWS \citep{Ren2019} and the genetic attack of \citet{Alzantot2018}, and score-based grey-box attacks such as DeepWordBug \citep{Gao2018}, TextFooler \citep{Jin2020} and BERT-Attack \citep{Li2020bert}. These better match the realistic deployment of credibility filters as third-party APIs. We base our work on the grey-box approach and include various modification of the output score (including black-boxing) as defence techniques. In terms of \textbf{defender's} access, we also assume the \textit{black-box} model, assuming the victim models deployment as third-party service.

\paragraph{Defences against textual AEs.}
Defensive techniques fall into three broad families \citep{Goyal2023survey, Qiu2022survey}.
\textbf{(i) White-box training-time defences} modify the victim via adversarial training \citep{Madry2018, Miyato2017advtrain}, certified robustness \citep{Jia2019cert, Ye2020safer}, or specialised loss terms \citep{Zhu2020freelb} --- all impractical for users of large hosted models.
\textbf{(ii) Detection-based defences} flag suspect inputs rather than correcting them \citep{Mozes2021fgws, Zhou2019disp}, at the cost of latency and false positives.
\textbf{(iii) Black-box wrap-around defences} transform either the input or the output of the victim before it reaches downstream consumers. On the \emph{input side}, examples include spell correction \citep{Pruthi2019combating}, randomised smoothing \citep{Ye2020safer}, character-level denoising \citep{Jones2020robenc}, and embedding-space robustification against claim edits \citep{Magomere2025claimrobust}; recent work has extended this strand to LLM-based input purification \citep{Moraffah2024purification, Robey2023smoothllm} and to adversarial-aware multi-agent filtering pipelines \citep{Aldahoul2025multiagent, Tong2025groupadaptive}. On the \emph{output side}, post-processing the classifier's probability vector --- argmax discretisation (returning only the predicted label), additive output noise, stochastic decision thresholds, or random label flips --- demotes the attacker's interface from grey-box to black-box at zero training cost. Such output-randomisation tactics have been analysed both as a generic obfuscation defence and as a target for adaptive attackers \citep{Tramer2020, Athalye2018}. This family is attractive because it requires no model access and can be wrapped around any classifier; we revisit and benchmark four output-side variants in Section~\ref{sec:static-defences}. The CLEF-2024 CheckThat! lab \citep{Barron2024checkthat} likewise added an adversarial-robustness track for credibility tasks, mirroring our motivation.

To our knowledge, no prior work in text classification has compared black-box defences systematically across tasks, victim architectures, and \emph{adaptive} attackers, nor combined them with a learned per-example defence selector. The closest prior benchmark \citep{Li2021defender} evaluates 11 word-substitution defences on classification and sentiment tasks, but covers only non-adaptive attackers and fixed defences. We close both gaps here, in the credibility-assessment setting.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Attack evaluation framework}
\label{sec:attack-framework}
We adopt the BODEGA evaluation protocol \cite{verifying_robustness}. The benchmark covers four English-language credibility tasks --- propaganda detection (\textbf{PR2}), fact-checking (\textbf{FC}), hyperpartisan news detection (\textbf{HN}), and rumour detection (\textbf{RD}) --- each detailed in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. Each task provides labelled training data and an evaluation set of inputs that the victim classifies correctly in the absence of attack. The attacker queries the victim $f(\cdot)$ and produces a perturbed input $x'$ from each original $x$. A trial is scored along three axes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attack success} --- whether the victim's prediction flipped, i.e. $f(x)\neq f(x')$ (binary, averaged into the attack success rate, ASR);
    \item \textbf{Semantic similarity} between $x$ and $x'$, measured with BLEURT \citep{Sellam2020};
    \item \textbf{Character similarity} between $x$ and $x'$, computed as the normalised Levenshtein distance \citep{1965}.
\end{itemize}
The composite \textit{BODEGA score} is the product of these three components, rewarding attacks that are simultaneously effective and unobtrusive. We measure attacker effectiveness by the average BODEGA score on each task--victim--defence--attacker combination; a failed attack scores zero. For each defence we also report clean accuracy on unattacked inputs. We cap each attack at 500 victim queries\footnote{\hl{This reduces the performance of some attackers, especially on longer text. However, it would be unrealistic to assume unlimited queries to the attacked service and previous research explored even stricter limits} \citep{Przybyla2025LLMattack}}.

\subsection{Static defences}
\label{sec:static-defences}
A static defence is a deterministic or stochastic transformation inserted in the victim's input ($t_\text{in}(\cdot)$) or output ($t_\text{out}(\cdot)$),  replacing $f(\cdot)$ with $t_\text{out}(f(t_\text{in}(\cdot)))$. We evaluate four mechanism families; Table~\ref{tab:action-gallery} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-action-gallery}) shows each defence applied to the same example.

\emph{Input cleaning:} \textbf{spellcheck} corrects OOV tokens against a SymSpell frequency dictionary; \textbf{unicode} applies NFKC normalisation, ASCII-maps confusable codepoints, and strips zero-width characters --- a direct counter to homoglyph attacks.

\emph{Input perturbation:} \textbf{char\_noise}$_\sigma$ substitutes each character with a Unicode homoglyph with probability $\sigma$, on the assumption that an attacker's crafted edits are more fragile to extra noise than natural language is.

\emph{Output masking:} \textbf{discretize} replaces the victim's probability vector with a one-hot, hiding the soft scores attackers exploit to estimate gradients (turning the attack into a black-box scenario).


\emph{Smoothing and combinations:} \textbf{majority\_vote@$k$} draws $k$ stochastic copies of the input and aggregates the victim's verdicts on them. Each copy is produced by independently perturbing every non-whitespace character with probability $p{=}0.1$, applying one of three edits chosen uniformly at random: deletion, swap with the next character, or insertion of a random lowercase letter. Rather than collapsing the $k$ verdicts to a hard label, we return the vote-fractions as a probability vector, so the API still exposes a continuous score but one that is quantised to $1/k$ and re-sampled afresh on every query --- a noisy oracle in the spirit of randomised smoothing \citep{Ye2020safer}. \textbf{spellcheck\_mv@$k$} composes spellcheck with this majority vote. We test $k \in \{3, 7\}$.

For each defence we report both clean accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:clean-accuracy}) and robustness against the four standard attackers (Table~\ref{tab:results-main}), making the trade-off explicit.

\subsection{Adaptive defences (MACABEU)}
\label{sec:macabeu}
Static defences are brittle once the attacker can adapt to a known transformation: \citet{Athalye2018} and \citet{Tramer2020} reduce, respectively, 7/9 and 13/13 published defences to baseline robustness with defence-specific attacks, and our RQ2 experiment (Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}) reproduces this for text. Yet the defender does not, in practice, know which attack a given example uses. We therefore introduce MACABEU, a meta-defender that selects which static defence to apply per example using reinforcement learning \citep{SuttonBarto2018} --- \hl{specifically a contextual bandit} \citep{LiContextualBandit2010} \hl{acting on input features, similar to XARELLO's attacker-side construction} \citep{przybyla-etal-2024-know}. Figure~\ref{fig:macabeu-arch} sketches the resulting loop.

  \label{fig:macabeu-arch}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Formulation.}
We frame defence selection as a contextual bandit \citep{LiContextualBandit2010, Langford2008}: each evaluation example is a single-step episode in which the agent observes a feature vector $\phi(x)$ of the input $x$, picks one defence $a\in\mathcal{A}$ to apply to it, and receives a scalar reward determined by whether the victim's prediction on the defended text matches the gold label.

The state $\phi(x)$ is a 10-dimensional vector of surface statistics: length, word count, average word length, OOV ratio, non-ASCII ratio, uppercase ratio, digit ratio, punctuation density, repeated-character ratio, and character entropy. Each is a known signature of a different attack family, so $\phi(x)$ carries enough signal for the policy to infer the attack and the defence likely to undo it.

The action space $\mathcal{A}$ contains eight defences from Section~\ref{sec:static-defences}, in the four mechanism families: \texttt{none} (pass-through), \texttt{spellcheck}, \texttt{unicode}, \texttt{majority\_vote@3}, \texttt{majority\_vote@7}, \texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3}, \texttt{discretize}, and \texttt{char\_noise@0.10}; Table~\ref{tab:action-gallery} (appendix \ref{sec:appendix-action-gallery}) shows each applied to the same example.


The reward is $r(a) = +1$ if the victim's prediction on the defended input matches the gold label and $-1$ otherwise, minus a small inference-cost penalty $c(a)$ that discourages over-using the most expensive defences ($c=0.10$ for $k{=}7$ smoothing, $0.05$ for $k{=}3$, $0.02$ for \texttt{char\_noise}, $0$ otherwise). The action-value network is a 3-layer MLP ($64$/$32$ hidden, ${\sim}3$k parameters) trained with $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration and Adam \citep{Kingma2015}.


\paragraph{}
We consider three operating regimes:
\paragraph{Offline (MACABEU-off).} The policy is trained on synthetic attacker--defender interactions over the training portion in BODEGA: for each training example we simulate a randomly chosen attacker, observe the outcome under each candidate defence, and update the action-value network. At deployment (attack portion) the policy is frozen.
\paragraph{Online.} The offline policy is used as a warm start, and the agent continues to update its network from real attacker queries observed at evaluation time, using a replay buffer of size 16 with a 50-example warm-up. The defender thus adapts to the specific attacker it is facing, mirroring the asymmetry assumed by adaptive attackers. \hl{This process requires access to true labels in order to signal the effectiveness of the defence. We consider two variants: assuming true labels are available during adaptation, e.g. from humans in the loop (\textbf{MACABEU-oracle}), or estimated by checking the victim's response to \texttt{majority\_vote@7} (\textbf{MACABEU-estimated})}. \hl{A direct comparison of the two variants on the subset of task--victim cells where the estimated variant finished within the revision budget is presented in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-macabeu-variants}; unless stated otherwise, MACABEU-on in the main text refers to the oracle variant to ensure comparability across the full 3-victim grid.}
% Note that MACABEU training requires access to true labels in order to signal the effectiveness of the defence. While we can assume they are always available for MACABEU-Off (trained on set-aside data), it is not necessarily so for MACABEU-On, retrained on incoming data. \section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
\paragraph{Tasks and data.}
We use the four tasks of text credibility assessment in English bundled with BODEGA \citep{verifying_robustness}: propaganda detection (\textbf{PR2}; based on SemEval-2020 Task 11 \citep{DaSanMartino2020semeval}), fact checking (\textbf{FC}; FEVER \citep{Thorne2018fever}), hyperpartisan news detection (\textbf{HN}; PAN at SemEval-2019 \citep{Kiesel2019hyperpartisan}), and rumour detection (\textbf{RD}; augmented RNR \citep{Zubiaga2016rumours}). Their training sizes differ greatly ($\sim$8.7k for RD up to $\sim$173k for FC; full per-split counts in Table~\ref{tab:tasks-stats}, appendix \ref{sec:appendix-task-stats}).

\paragraph{Victim classifiers.}
To probe whether defences transfer across architectures, we train three victims per task: a \textbf{BiLSTM} over 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings, a fine-tuned \textbf{BERT-base}, and a QLoRA-fine-tuned \textbf{Gemma-2B}. They differ in parameter count and cover the dominant paradigms in current text classification systems.

\paragraph{Attackers.}
We re-use the four standard black-box attackers and the adaptive attacker XARELLO introduced in Section~\ref{sec:related-work}; BERT-Attack, PWWS, DeepWordBug, Genetic, XARELLO, each capped at 500 victim queries per example.

\paragraph{Protocols.}
We conduct three experimental blocks. \textbf{RQ1:} the twelve static defences against the four standard attackers on every task--victim combination (Gemma restricted to the eight strongest defences identified on BiLSTM and BERT to limit computational resources), with clean-accuracy cost. \textbf{RQ2:} XARELLO against the strongest static defences on every combination, isolating attacker adaptivity. \textbf{RQ3:} \hl{MACABEU variants against all five attackers on every combination --- $3 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3 = 180$ combinations, with GEMMA excluded for MACABEU-estimated (see Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-macabeu-variants})} --- isolating defender adaptivity (vs.\ RQ2) and the gain of an adaptive defender over the best static defence (vs.\ RQ1).


\paragraph{Implementation.}
Hardware, hyperparameters, and library versions are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-impl}.

\subsection{RQ1: Static defences}
\label{sec:results-rq1}

\paragraph{Aggregated robustness.}
Table~\ref{tab:results-main} reports the mean BODEGA score for each $\langle$defence, attacker$\rangle$ pair, aggregated over all 12 task--victim sub-combinations (4 tasks $\times$ 3 victims). Focusing on the four standard attacker columns (BERT-Attack, PWWS, DeepWordBug, Genetic), three clear patterns emerge.

\textbf{(i) Spellcheck foils character-level attacks but misses word-level ones.} Against DeepWordBug, \texttt{spellcheck} drops mean BODEGA from $0.259$ (undefended) to $0.087$ --- a $66\%$ reduction. Against the three word-level attackers (BERT-Attack, PWWS, Genetic) it is essentially inconsequential (mean BODEGA $0.448$/$0.366$/$0.344$ vs.\ $0.445$/$0.357$/$0.376$ undefended). The pattern is uniform across all victims.

\textbf{(ii) Output masking uniformly weakens all four attackers.} Hiding the victim's soft probabilities (\texttt{discretize}) cuts mean BODEGA from $0.359$ to $0.241$ --- a $33\%$ reduction --- and the per-attacker drop is similar in every column (BERT-Attack $-34\%$, PWWS $-27\%$, DeepWordBug $-31\%$, Genetic $-39\%$), with no input transformation applied.

\textbf{(iii) Ensembling dominates among single-action defences.} Majority-voting and its spell-corrected variant (\texttt{MV@3}, \texttt{MV@7}, \texttt{sc\_mv@3}) all bring mean BODEGA below $0.14$ across the four standard attackers, with \texttt{sc\_mv@3} the strongest at $0.123$ mean. \texttt{char\_noise@0.10} achieves comparable robustness ($0.138$) at a higher clean-accuracy cost (Table~\ref{tab:clean-accuracy}).


\paragraph{Clean-accuracy cost.}
Table~\ref{tab:clean-accuracy} reports clean accuracy on unattacked inputs, averaged across the three victims, with each non-baseline cell annotated by its percentage change relative to the undefended baseline. Input-perturbing defences pay the largest tax: \texttt{char\_noise@0.10} loses $8.3\%$ relative to baseline ($0.715$ vs.\ $0.780$), and the majority-voting variants lose $7$--$9\%$. \texttt{spellcheck} ($+0.8\%$) and \texttt{unicode} ($-0.1\%$) are accuracy-preserving, and \texttt{discretize} is identical to the baseline by construction (it touches only the output). The offline MACABEU policy, free to pick \texttt{none} on benign inputs, attains the highest mean clean accuracy ($0.790$, $+1.3\%$). Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} plots all eleven policies on the accuracy--robustness plane. MACABEU-off sits at the top-right corner: it yields the highest clean accuracy and cuts mean BODEGA by $55\%$ relative to \texttt{none} ($0.160$ vs.\ $0.359$). The reason is structural: when no attack is present, the policy falls back to the accuracy-preserving defences in its action library; under attack, it switches to the more aggressive transforms that trade some accuracy for robustness. We unpack this behaviour in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}. \hl{The two online variants push BODEGA further down at a modest accuracy cost: MACABEU-oracle to $0.097$ ($-1.5\%$ accuracy vs.\ baseline), and MACABEU-estimated to $0.133$ ($-4.2\%$ accuracy) --- both Pareto-dominate the static ensemble defences (\texttt{sc\_mv@3} at $0.123$ BODEGA and $-8.2\%$ accuracy).}

\paragraph{Best static defences going forward.}
We select the strongest static defences from the aggregated grid for RQ2/RQ3. Among them, \texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3} is the single strongest defence on mean BODEGA and the natural target for an adaptive attacker.

\subsection{RQ2: Adaptive attacks}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

\paragraph{Pretrained XARELLO vs.\ static defences.}
The rightmost column of Table~\ref{tab:results-main} reports XARELLO's mean BODEGA under three defences for which we have run the adaptive attacker on all 12 task--victim combinations. Against the undefended victim XARELLO scores $0.585$, well above any standard attacker, confirming the gap between adaptive and non-adaptive search. Wrapping the same victim in \texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3} --- the strongest static defence on the standard-attacker grid --- only reduces XARELLO's mean BODEGA to $0.374$. By contrast, the standard attackers all collapse to $\leq 0.16$ under the same defence: a $\sim 60\%$ gap between adaptive and non-adaptive damage at equal defence.

\paragraph{Retraining against a stochastic defence weakens the adaptive attacker.}
A defence in the adaptive threat model should be tested against an attacker retrained with the defence in place. Table~\ref{tab:xarello-adapt} reports this: a second adaptive agent, retrained from scratch against \texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3}, is evaluated alongside the pretrained one on both undefended and defended deployments. On the defended deployment the retrained agent is worse than the pretrained one in 6 of 8 task--victim combinations (mean BODEGA $0.256$ vs.\ $0.305$); only PR2-BERT and, marginally, HN-BERT improve --- both combinations where the pretrained attacker was already crushed by the defence ($<\!0.2$ BODEGA), leaving so little signal that any retraining drift registers as gain. We attribute the overall collapse to the defence's stochasticity: three random perturbations re-encode every query, so the attacker's $Q$-network ends up fitting to noise rather than a stable victim.

\subsection{RQ3: Adaptive defences}
\label{sec:results-rq3}

\paragraph{MACABEU vs.\ adaptive attacker.}

  \setlength{\abovecaptionskip}{4pt}

The XARELLO column of Table~\ref{tab:results-main} reports mean BODEGA for an undefended victim ($0.585$), MACABEU-Off ($0.542$), and MACABEU-On ($0.356$). The offline policy barely improves on the baseline ($-0.043$) and is in fact weaker than \texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3} ($0.374$); the online policy cuts BODEGA by $0.229$ ($39\%$ relative), below any static defence. The offline--online contrast is the central RQ3 result: adaptivity, not policy capacity, closes the gap. Per-combination, online MACABEU wins in 12/12 (Figure~\ref{fig:headline}), $16$--$56\%$ below the offline policy.

\paragraph{MACABEU vs.\ standard attackers.}
The first four columns of Table~\ref{tab:results-main} tell the same story against the standard attackers. Online MACABEU has the lowest mean BODEGA in every column. The offline version uses the same eight actions and is beaten by \texttt{sc\_mv@3} in every column, so the win comes from updating during the attack, not from a stronger library.

\hl{MACABEU-oracle is a theoretical upper bound since it needs the gold label at inference time. Its deployment-realistic counterpart, MACABEU-estimated, uses a MajorityVote-7 pseudo-label instead and approaches the oracle on character-level attackers (BERTattack and XARELLO, within 5\% of oracle); on word-level ones (PWWS, Genetic) it loses more ground ($+60\%$ and $+27\%$). Estimated still beats every static defence. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-macabeu-variants} has the per-attacker numbers.}


\paragraph{What does the online policy learn?}
Figure~\ref{fig:heatmaps-combined} shows online MACABEU-oracle's post-warmup action distribution by attacker (left) and by task (right). With no attacker, \texttt{spellcheck} accounts for $47\%$ of selections and \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} for only $6\%$; under attack these flip. Within the attacker columns three patterns stand out. \emph{(i)} \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} dominates against all four standard attackers ($20$--$34\%$): each commits to a fixed search procedure, and a randomised input transform invalidates the importance scores it caches across queries. \emph{(ii)} Against DeepWordBug --- the only character-level attacker --- \texttt{spellcheck} ($20\%$) and \texttt{unicode} ($18\%$) rise close to \texttt{char\_noise@0.1}, since they specifically reverse typos and homoglyph-like edits. \emph{(iii)} Under XARELLO the policy shifts away from randomised defences: \texttt{spellcheck} becomes the most-selected action ($22\%$) and \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} drops to $16\%$. XARELLO's $Q$-network averages many queries per example, so a stochastic transform feeds it noise but a deterministic cleaner cannot be averaged out. The task panel mirrors this: \textbf{PR2/FC} concentrate on \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} ($27$--$31\%$); \textbf{HN} concentrates on \texttt{spellcheck} ($28\%$) with \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} dropping to $7\%$ --- hyperpartisan labels hinge on charged vocabulary that random noise corrupts; \textbf{RD} is bimodal (\texttt{spellcheck} $22\%$, \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} $20\%$), short tweets giving both transforms high per-edit leverage. The pattern is not explained by clean-accuracy cost: \texttt{char\_noise@0.1} costs PR2 $-2.5\%$ vs.\ FC $-14.9\%$, yet both rely on it heavily.

Tables~\ref{tab:qualitative-bilstm}--\ref{tab:qualitative-gemma} (appendix \ref{sec:appendix-extra-figs}) make this concrete: online MACABEU forces XARELLO from a few high-leverage word substitutions into many low-impact character-level edits, degrading both semantic and character similarity on every combination.

\hl{Additionally, we perform a feature importance analysis, indicating that MACABEU mostly relies on text length (in characters and words), as well as the ratio of uppercase and out-of-vocabulary words to choose the right defence. The details are in appendix} \ref{sec:featureimportance}. 

\section{Discussion}

A single static defence creates a stationary problem that an adaptive attacker eventually solves. Allowing the defender to be adaptive as well restores the symmetry: every attacker action becomes a learning signal for the defender. Online MACABEU exploits exactly this, where the win is not due to a stronger defence library (it shares its eight actions with the offline policy) but a better policy over the same library, learned against the deployed attacker. The qualitative ranking of static defences is preserved across BiLSTM, BERT, and Gemma (ensembles dominate; character-level cleaning helps only against character-level attacks; output masking buys a constant fraction), and the online MACABEU win is victim-uniform (BERT $-41\%$, Gemma $-33\%$; the latter reflects Gemma being intrinsically harder to attack). \hl{MACABEU-oracle is a theoretical upper bound since it needs the gold label at inference time. MACABEU-estimated replaces that reward with a MV7 pseudo-label and approaches the oracle's performance without any label access: mean BODEGA is only $\sim$20\% higher on the BiLSTM+BERT sub-grid, and still below every static defence (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-macabeu-variants}). The gap is larger on word-level attackers (PWWS $+60\%$, Genetic $+27\%$), where substitutions do not break the MV7 vote and the pseudo-label reinforces the wrong action.}

Black-box preprocessing nonetheless has a hard floor: even under online MACABEU, XARELLO retains a residual attack success rate (ASR) of 15--76\% across all combinations. When the attacker produces semantically faithful, character-clean perturbations that genuinely flip the victim's decision boundary, no input-side transformation can recover the original prediction. Closing the gap requires complementary defences --- input authenticity, provenance, or victim-side modification --- which fall outside our threat model. Cost-wise, the strongest static defences (\texttt{sc\_mv@7}, \texttt{char\_noise@0.1}) are also the most expensive; online MACABEU's per-query overhead (one Adam step on a 3k-parameter network) is negligible relative to the victim forward pass it wraps.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented the first systematic comparison of black-box preprocessing defences for misinformation detection across four tasks, three victim architectures, and five attackers, and introduced MACABEU, an RL-based meta-defender that selects defences per example and keeps learning during the attack. Across the resulting 12 task--victim combinations, online MACABEU reduces an adaptive attacker's BODEGA score by $16$--$56\%$ relative to its offline counterpart, without modifying the victim. We release all code and policies in the hope that adaptive defence --- cheap to retrofit onto already-deployed classifiers --- becomes a routine companion to the systems it protects.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{(i) Language coverage.} All experiments are restricted to English-language credibility datasets, and the SymSpell dictionary, BLEURT model, and pretrained victims are likewise English-specific. The MACABEU architecture itself is language-agnostic --- the action library only requires per-language analogues of the underlying transforms --- so extending the study to multilingual misinformation corpora would be feasible.

\paragraph{(ii) Hand-curated action space.} MACABEU's eight actions are static, character- or token-level preprocessing primitives. Two natural directions for richer libraries are paraphrase- and purification-based defences, where an LLM rewrites the input \citep{Moraffah2024purification, Robey2023smoothllm}, and learned token-substitution defences such as robust encodings \citep{Jones2020robenc} or adversarial typo correction \citep{Pruthi2019combating}. Because MACABEU's selection is decoupled from the action implementations, such defences can be added as additional arms without retraining the meta-controller.

\paragraph{(iii) Single-attacker assumption.} Online MACABEU is trained against a single attacker per session and assumes the attacker's strategy is approximately stationary across queries. An adversary that switches attack styles mid-session, or that coordinates several attackers against the same defender, would induce a non-stationary environment for the online learner. Mitigations that maintain ensembles of frozen policies and switch between them at detected change points, or that lower the effective learning rate when reward variance spikes, are directly compatible with our architecture and a natural follow-up.

\paragraph{(iv) Compute scope.} To keep total GPU usage tractable on a single-GPU SLURM cluster, we deliberately restricted two of the heaviest sub-grids. Gemma was evaluated only against the top eight static defences identified in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}, since each Gemma attack run is roughly an order of magnitude more expensive than its BERT or BiLSTM counterpart; the standard-attacker grid for the seven discarded defences is dominated by ensemble defences that are already weaker than \texttt{sc\_mv@3}, so the omission does not affect the qualitative ranking. Retrained-XARELLO results are similarly restricted to BiLSTM (Table~\ref{tab:xarello-adapt}), where each retraining run is cheapest; the pretrained-XARELLO grid against MACABEU-oracle is complete across all 12 task--victim combinations. \hl{MACABEU-estimated was evaluated on BiLSTM and BERT but not on Gemma. The MV7 pseudo-label reward needs seven extra base-victim forward passes per online update, which pushed each RD--Gemma cell past a single-GPU week. Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-macabeu-variants} therefore reports estimated on the BiLSTM+BERT sub-grid only.}

\paragraph{(v) Semantic-similarity proxy.} BODEGA uses BLEURT \citep{Sellam2020} as its single semantic-similarity component. Alternative proxies (BERTScore, COMET, human ratings) could shift the absolute BODEGA scale; we expect the qualitative comparisons between defences to remain stable, but a robustness study across metric choices would strengthen the claim.

\paragraph{(vi) Black-box scope.} Our threat model excludes any modification to the victim model, which is the right setting for retrofitting defences onto already-deployed classifiers but leaves complementary lines of work --- adversarial training of the victim \citep{Madry2018, Miyato2017advtrain}, input authenticity checks, and provenance signals --- outside the scope of this study.

\paragraph{(vii) Query budget.} Each attacker is allowed up to 500 queries per example, which is higher than what a deployed misinformation classifier would typically expose: real platforms aggressively rate-limit queries and observe only a small fraction of them. Approaches that explicitly bound the attacker's query budget, such as TREPAT \citep{Przybyla2025LLMattack}, are a natural complement to adaptive preprocessing and would tighten the threat model used here.

\paragraph{(viii) Victim scale.} Our victims --- a BiLSTM trained from scratch, BERT-base fine-tuned in full, and Gemma-2B with QLoRA --- are small by 2026 production standards. Real deployments increasingly rely on multi-billion-parameter LLMs, whose decision surfaces, vulnerability profile, and clean-accuracy budget under a given preprocessing defence may differ from what we observe at this scale. A follow-up evaluating MACABEU against larger victim backbones (Gemma-7B/27B, Llama-3 8B/70B) would be necessary to extrapolate our quantitative claims.

\paragraph{(ix) Fine-tuning vs.\ prompting.} All three victims are fine-tuned task-specific classifiers. Prompting-based misinformation detection --- where the same backbone is instructed in-context rather than fine-tuned on the BODEGA training split --- is increasingly common in practice and exhibits a different robustness profile (in particular, prompt injection becomes a parallel attack surface). MACABEU is in principle backbone-agnostic, but its action library, learning rate, and warm-up budget were tuned for fine-tuned victims and would have to be re-validated under prompting.

\paragraph{\hl{(x) Pseudo-label quality for online adaptation.}} \hl{MACABEU-estimated uses MajorityVote-7 on the defended input as the reward signal instead of the gold label. This is a good proxy for character-level attackers: the perturbations tend to break the attack and MV7's vote lines up with the true label. It is a poor proxy for word-level attackers (PWWS, Genetic): synonym substitutions survive the perturbation, so MV7 agrees with the fooled prediction and the reward pushes the policy the wrong way. Better pseudo-labels are a natural next step --- for example, paraphrase or back-translation for an orthogonal signal, delayed user signals (report rates, moderation actions) in a deployed system, or human review triggered only on uncertain cases.}

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

\paragraph{Dual-use risk.} The defences we study are explicitly designed to harden misinformation classifiers deployed against adversarial users. The same action library --- spell-correction, Unicode normalisation, majority-vote averaging, character noise, output discretisation --- is, however, equally inspectable by an attacker who wishes to design perturbations that survive a given defence. We argue that the asymmetry favours defenders in this setting: the offline MACABEU policy and all static defences are already accessible to a black-box attacker through query observation (Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}), so disclosure does not materially expand the attacker's surface; conversely, the online MACABEU regime --- which is the central contribution --- is, by construction, only usable by an operator with control of the inference pipeline and therefore strengthens defenders disproportionately.

%\paragraph{Datasets and PII.} All four BODEGA tasks (PR2, FC, HN, RD) are publicly released research corpora derived from previously published news, fact-checking, and rumour-detection collections. We use them in their released form and introduce no new annotations of individuals or private content; no personally identifying information was scraped, augmented, or redistributed as part of this work.

\paragraph{Misuse of misinformation detectors.} Robust credibility classifiers can in principle also be used to over-filter legitimate dissenting speech. Our experiments measure robustness to adversarial perturbation, not the calibration of any particular deployment decision; the defences studied here neither shift the original classifier's decision threshold (with the exception of \texttt{discretize}, whose effect is bounded and reported) nor change the set of inputs that the underlying victim labels as non-credible. Decisions about how to act on a classifier's output remain the responsibility of the operator.

\paragraph{Reproducibility and AI assistance.} We release all code, MACABEU policies, and configuration under a permissive licence, alongside the SLURM harnesses needed to reproduce the experiments on a single-GPU node. As disclosed in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-impl}, the implementation was developed with the help of LLM-based coding assistants; all generated code was reviewed, tested, and validated by the authors.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{sec:appendix-impl}

\paragraph{Hardware and tooling.} All experiments are run on a single-GPU SLURM cluster (NVIDIA A40/L40s, 48\,GB). Victims and attackers follow the BODEGA reference implementation \citep{verifying_robustness}: BiLSTM victims are trained from scratch on the BODEGA training splits over 300-dimensional GloVe embeddings \citep{Pennington2014}; BERT-base \citep{Devlin2019} is fine-tuned for 3 epochs; Gemma-2B \citep{Gemma2024} is fine-tuned with QLoRA \citep{Dettmers2023} (rank 16, $\alpha=32$, 4-bit NF4 quantisation). All attackers are wrapped through OpenAttack \citep{Zeng2021} with a 500-query budget per example. Semantic similarity uses BLEURT-20 \citep{Sellam2020, Pu2021}; character similarity uses normalised Levenshtein distance \citep{1965} via the \texttt{editdistance} library; spellchecking uses SymSpell with the standard 80k-word English frequency dictionary; Unicode confusable mappings come from the \texttt{homoglyphs} library and the official Unicode confusables table. Code, configurations, and trained MACABEU policies are released alongside the paper.

\paragraph{Compute budget.} Experiments are carried out on a shared SLURM cluster, with each job allocated a single NVIDIA A40 or L40s GPU (48\,GB). The full evaluation comprises 4 tasks $\times$ 3 victim classifiers $\times$ 12 defences $\times$ 5 attackers, plus MACABEU offline training (one run per task--victim combination) and online policy evaluation against XARELLO. Victim fine-tuning (BERT, Gemma) and BLEURT-based BODEGA scoring dominate the per-job cost; per-action MACABEU updates contribute a negligible fraction. Across all completed runs reported here and in development, the project consumed approximately \textbf{3{,}400 GPU-hours} (1{,}776 GPU jobs).

\paragraph{Software versions.} The experiments use Python 3.10.20 with PyTorch 2.7.1 (CUDA 11.8, cuDNN 9.1), Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} 4.46.3, \texttt{tokenizers} 0.20.3, and \texttt{datasets} 4.7.0. Adversarial attacks run through OpenAttack 2.1.1; semantic similarity uses \texttt{bleurt-pytorch} 0.0.1 (BLEURT-20 checkpoint) and \texttt{bert-score} 0.3.13; character similarity uses \texttt{editdistance} 0.8.1; and the static-defence library uses \texttt{symspellpy} 6.9.0 (English 80k-word frequency dictionary) and \texttt{homoglyphs} 2.0.4. Gemma fine-tuning relies on \texttt{peft} 0.18.1, \texttt{bitsandbytes} 0.49.2, and \texttt{accelerate} 1.13.0; numerical code uses NumPy 1.26.4.

\paragraph{Reporting and variability.} All reported BODEGA scores correspond to single attacker runs with fixed random seeds across defence, attacker, task, and victim seeds; we do not average over multiple repetitions per (defence, attacker, task, victim) cell, given the size of the evaluation matrix. The MACABEU \emph{online} numbers carry inherent run-to-run variance from the per-query replay buffer and $\varepsilon$-greedy exploration; the consistency of the 12/12 win pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:headline} suggests this variance is small relative to the offline--online gap, but a multi-seed study would be needed to quantify it.


\paragraph{MACABEU state features.} The state vector encodes ten features of the current input:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Length, in characters.
    \item Word count (whitespace-tokenised).
    \item Average word length.
    \item Out-of-vocabulary ratio: fraction of whitespace tokens not found in the SymSpell English frequency dictionary.
    \item Non-ASCII character ratio.
    \item Uppercase character ratio.
    \item Digit ratio.
    \item Punctuation density.
    \item Repeated-character ratio: fraction of length-3 substrings consisting of three identical characters, normalised by input length.
    \item Shannon entropy of the character distribution.
\end{enumerate}


%\paragraph{MACABEU reward shaping.} $r = +1$ on correct prediction, $-1$ otherwise, minus a per-action cost: \texttt{none}/\texttt{spellcheck}/\texttt{unicode}/\texttt{discretize} = 0; \texttt{majority\_vote@3}/\texttt{spellcheck\_mv@3} = 0.05; \texttt{majority\_vote@7} = 0.10; \texttt{char\_noise@0.10} = 0.02.

\paragraph{MACABEU hyperparameters.} Offline: replay buffer 50k, batch 64, $\eta=10^{-3}$ (Adam), target update every 200 steps, $\gamma=0$, 200 training episodes per task--victim. Online: warm-up 50 examples at $\varepsilon=0.5$, replay buffer 16, batch 16, one update per query post-warmup. Initial $\varepsilon$ in offline mode anneals from 1.0 to 0.05 over training.


\paragraph{Use of AI coding assistants.} The implementation --- training and evaluation scripts, data-conversion utilities, SLURM job harnesses, and plotting/table-generation tooling --- was developed with the help of LLM-based coding assistants (Claude Code with Opus 4.6 and 4.7). All generated code was reviewed, tested, and integrated by the authors; experimental design, hyperparameter choices, analyses, and the paper text remain our own.

Two things stand out. \section{Feature importance analysis}
\label{sec:featureimportance}

Section~\ref{sec:macabeu} lists the 10 features MACABEU uses to pick a defence. To see which ones actually matter, we swap each feature's value in a given example for the dataset mean of that feature, and check whether the greedy action changes. A feature is important if this swap flips the action often. We report the \emph{flip rate} per feature: the fraction of examples where the action changed after the swap.

We run this on every trained policy on disk: one offline policy per (task, victim), and one online oracle and one online estimated policy per (task, victim, attacker). This gives 880 (policy, feature) flip rates in total.

Figure~\ref{fig:featimp-agg} averages the flip rate across all policies within each variant. Four features do most of the work: \emph{text length} (0.18--0.26 flip rate), \emph{uppercase ratio} (0.13--0.26), \emph{word count} (0.13--0.22), and \emph{OOV ratio} (0.14--0.23). The other six stay below $0.11$ and barely influence the choice.

Two smaller findings. The offline policy has roughly double the flip rates of either online variant on the four dominant features --- the frozen policy leans hard on single features, and online adaptation spreads the decision across more of them. And MACABEU-oracle and MACABEU-estimated agree on the per-feature flip rates within $0.02$ on average, so the MV7 pseudo-label ends up using the same features as the gold label, just with a weaker reward signal.

Figure~\ref{fig:featimp-atk} breaks this down by attacker for the two online variants. The four dominant features stay dominant everywhere. DeepWordBug pushes text length higher on the long-text tasks (RD, HN), because character-level insertions inflate the input. XARELLO spreads its edits more, so the policy against XARELLO uses the four features more evenly.
